Title: Traditional Community Seed Banks
Category: Food Security / Biodiversity / Climate Adaptation
Problem Solved:
Preserving genetic crop diversity, protecting local farming systems from seed monopolies, and maintaining access to climate-resilient local crop varieties.

Historical Origin:
For millennia, agricultural societies practiced seed selection and storage from harvest to harvest, sharing seeds within communities. In the late 20th century, organized community-based seed banks emerged as a counter-movement to protect biodiversity from commercial agriculture.

How It Worked:
Farmers select seeds from plants that show resilience to local stress factors (like drought, flood, or pests) during the growing season. These seeds are preserved locally using traditional methods (such as storage in clay pots, mixing with protective ash or neem leaves, or drying in the sun). The seed bank functions like a library: farmers borrow seeds for the sowing season and return a larger quantity of seeds after a successful harvest, maintaining a local genetic repository.

Why It Was Forgotten:
The green revolution introduced high-yielding commercial hybrid seeds and genetically modified crop varieties. These seeds, which must be purchased annually along with chemical fertilizers and pesticides, displaced native, open-pollinated traditional seeds.

Modern Revival:
Community seed banks are being established worldwide as decentralized climate adaptation measures. They allow farmers to access seeds that have adapted to their specific local microclimates and changing rainfall patterns over generations, securing local food sovereignty.

Limitations:
They require robust community trust, organization, and technical training. Poor storage conditions (such as high humidity or pest infestation) can quickly ruin stored seeds and diminish seed viability.
