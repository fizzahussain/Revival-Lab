Title: Inca Terrace Farming (Andenes)
Category: Agriculture / Water Conservation / Soil Preservation
Problem Solved:
Farming successfully on steep mountain slopes, preventing soil erosion, conserving rainwater, and mitigating frost risk at high elevations.

Historical Origin:
Developed to an advanced scale by the Inca Empire (and earlier Andean cultures) in the rugged Andes mountains of South America, enabling agriculture across varying altitudes and steep terrains.

How It Worked:
Andenes are artificial agricultural terraces carved into hillsides and supported by stone retaining walls. The structure of each terrace consists of multiple layers: a base of large stones and gravel for drainage, a middle layer of sand, and a top layer of fertile topsoil. The stone retaining walls absorb heat from solar radiation during the day and radiate that heat back to the soil during freezing nights, creating a warmer microclimate that protects crops (like maize and potatoes) from high-altitude frost. Runoff rainwater is channeled systematically from the top terraces down to the lower ones, preventing soil erosion.

Why It Was Forgotten:
After the Spanish conquest of Peru in the 16th century, population collapse and forced resettlement of indigenous communities led to a lack of maintenance for the complex terraces. Modern agriculture favored flat valleys where large-scale machinery could be deployed.

Modern Revival:
Andean communities, NGOs, and governments are restoring ancient Inca terraces to improve climate resilience, combat landslides, optimize water conservation during droughts, and support sustainable, local crop production in mountainous terrains.

Limitations:
Building and maintaining agricultural terraces is highly labor-intensive, requiring manual stone-laying. The steep and narrow steps prevent the use of standard modern mechanical farming equipment.
